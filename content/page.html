Page one — the decision

Two systems, two jobs

You named it exactly right, so let's build on your words: credits are trust, control and perceived value. Billing is unit economics, revenue and profit. They are different machines and must not be the same machine.

The credit system's job

Make people feel in control

Predictable prices. A visible balance. Nothing charged for failure. A clear "this will cost X" before anything runs.

Credits are a trust interface. If someone is ever surprised by a charge, the credit system has failed — even when the accounting was perfectly correct.

The billing system's job

Make the company survive

Recurring revenue. Gross margin. Cash collection. Deferred-revenue accounting. Dunning. Tax.

Billing is a finance machine. People should barely see it — and when they do, it should be one clean invoice.

What this document is called

A Monetization PRD. That is the industry name. Call the file MONETIZATION.md and keep it beside your other handoffs.

The one decision everything hangs off

The call

You have one fixed cost that does not care how much anyone uses the product — the $500-a-month guardian. A pure pay-as-you-go credit model cannot carry a fixed cost: it only earns when someone is active, and the cost runs whether they are active or not.

So: a two-part tariff. A subscription floor pays for the fixed costs — guardians, storage, support, the always-on parts. Metered credits pay for the variable costs — model tokens, renders, transcription, egress — with margin on top.

Credits never carry the fixed cost. The subscription never carries the variable cost. Each part covers what it is shaped like.

The trap to avoid The commonest failure in credit products is letting the credit price float with your cost — "this run cost us more, so it costs you more." It destroys trust and makes the product impossible to budget for. Fix the price; manage the cost. Page three shows how to do that even though your four models differ in cost by ten times.

Page two — what to measure

The North Star, and its six branches

A North Star has to be a number that cannot go up unless the business is really working. Revenue can rise while you destroy trust. Signups can rise while you burn cash. For a credit product, one number resists both.

North Star

Weekly Successful Credit Burn

Credits settled on jobs that completed and were kept — not discarded, not refunded — summed every week.

It only rises when three things are true at once: people are active, they are doing work valuable enough to keep, and you are getting paid.

It cannot be gamed by breaking things, because failed jobs are free by policy. And it leads revenue by two to four weeks, so it doubles as an early warning.

The six branches

Each one has a single number you can move next week.

Branch 1

Acquisition

Signups, cost to acquire, and the share of them who reach a first GO within a day. Most people who never finish one job never come back.

Target — 60% of signups reach a first GO on day one
Branch 2

Activation

Time to First GO — minutes from sign-in to the first job that actually finishes. This is the single best predictor of whether someone ever pays you.

Target — median under 8 minutes
Branch 3

Engagement

Weekly active Prime members, live Scircles, and GOs per active per week. Below four a week they are visiting, not playing.

Target — 12 GOs per active per week
Branch 4

Consumption

Burn depth — how much of the monthly allotment actually gets used. Under 30% they will churn. Over 95% they are rationing, and rationing people stop exploring.

Healthy band — 60% to 80% of the allotment
Branch 5

Economics

Cost per credit, margin per credit, and accounts per guardian. The guardian share is the second-biggest line in a Prime account — and the only one you control by operations, not code.

Target — $0.002 cost per credit · 80% margin · 80+ accounts per guardian
Branch 6

Retention

Net revenue retention, logo churn, and the refund-and-waste rate. When a cohort rises back above where it started, expansion is beating churn — and you have a business that compounds.

Target — NRR above 110% · waste below 2%

Guardrails you are never allowed to trade away

A North Star creates pressure. These must hold while you push it — if one breaks, the gain was borrowed from next quarter.

Page three — credits

What every action costs

The anchor

1 credit = $0.01 of list value. 100 credits = $1. Plans buy credits below list, so the plan always beats paying as you go — that is your upgrade engine.

Our target cost is $0.002 per credit. Every price is set at 5× our cost, which is an 80% margin. If a new action can't be priced at 5× its cost, it doesn't ship until it can.

The model problem, and the clean fix

Your agents-data.js offers four models whose cost differs by ten times for the identical action. Charge one flat price and either Haiku users are overcharged or Fable users bankrupt you. So give each model a multiplier that tracks cost exactly — then margin comes out at 80% whichever model the person picks, and you can let them choose freely.

Two changes this implies (a) Your default is Opus 5 — a ×2.5 multiplier on the most common action in the product. Make Sonnet 5 the default and let Deep be a deliberate choice on the GO card. Worth roughly $5,700 a month at the month-12 scale, and it is one line. (b) Turn on prompt caching for Scircle and room context — your most repeated payload, at about a tenth the price when cached.

The GO gate

You already named this: NOW decides whether the scripting is possible. That is the authorisation gate, and every metered action walks it.

The hard floor Never sell a credit below $0.004 — 2× cost, 50% margin. That is the lowest an enterprise negotiation may reach. Write it down before your first big customer asks for a discount, because in the room you will want to say yes.

Page four — billing and the fixed cost

Money in, revenue later

Billing is a different machine with a different rhythm: per cycle, in months. The thing most founders get wrong is that taking money is not the same as earning it.

The three calls you own

Gateway

Paddle now, Stripe later

Paddle (~5% + $0.50) becomes the legal seller and handles worldwide VAT, GST and US sales tax. You cannot afford global tax compliance as a team of one.

Stripe Billing (2.9% + $0.30 + ~0.5%) has native meters, credit grants and entitlements — the closest match to this model. Move there when someone owns finance.

Razorpay if the entity is Indian — UPI AutoPay is the recurring rail that actually works there.

Currency

Credits inside, local prices outside

One internal unit of account: USD, because your costs are in USD.

The ledger holds credits, never currency. A euro customer and a rupee customer burn the identical credit, so your margin analysis needs no conversion at all.

Sell at fixed local price points — $39 / €39 / £33 / ₹2,999 — re-priced quarterly. Live FX gives you prices like €36.42, which reads as careless.

Legal shape

Credits are not money

Structure them as non-refundable, non-transferable, expiring service usage rights.

Never cash-out, peer transfer, a published redemption guarantee, or credits with no expiry. Each of those edges you toward being regulated as a payments business.

Have a lawyer in your billing jurisdiction read the credit terms before the first payment. It is a one-hour conversation that is very hard to have retroactively.

Why the $10 minimum top-up isn't arbitrary At 2.9% + $0.30, a $5 top-up costs you $0.45 — nine percent. A $2 top-up costs 18% and is pure loss. Floor it at $10, push auto-recharge in $25 and $50 blocks, and your processing line drops by about a third.

The $500 guardian — your one fixed cost

Everything else in your cost base moves with usage. This does not: $500 goes out whether that guardian did one job or ten thousand. That single fact set the whole model on page one.

One number to manage

Accounts per Guardian. A Prime account contributes about $30 a month before guardian cost. So 17 accounts on one guardian is break-even, 50 is the alert floor, and 80+ is where the margin you actually want lives.

A dedicated guardian is not a feature you include — it is a SKU you sell at $899 a month. Shared pools amortise the fixed cost; dedicated capacity gets charged for.

Four rules for guardian capacity

  1. Never provision on hope. Add a guardian only when $1,600/month of attributed revenue is committed behind it — a 2.5× coverage ratio. Queue depth is not a reason; revenue is.
  2. Measure coverage monthly. Gross profit attributed to that guardian ÷ $500. Target ≥ 2.5×, warn < 1.5×, act < 1.0× twice — consolidate its accounts and retire it.
  3. Hibernate idle dedicated guardians. Under 10% utilisation for 14 days, sleep it and prorate the next invoice. They will love you for it, and you stop paying for air.
  4. Rebalance pools weekly. Keep every pool inside 60–120 accounts. Below 60 you burn margin; above 120 you buy queue latency that shows up as churn.
This whole page rests on one assumption I have assumed one guardian serves many accounts at once, with a ceiling near 120. If a guardian is one-to-one with a customer — or is a person with a real hour budget — then accounts-per-guardian collapses to 1 and every price here has to roughly triple. It is the first question on page five.

Why A fails · 1

Free costs real money

12,000 free accounts burning 380 credits a month costs $9,120 — more than double what the payers cost. In a subscription model the free tier is a capped marketing expense. In a pure meter it is uncapped.

Why A fails · 2

Fixed costs have nothing to attach to

Sixteen guardians cost $8,000 whether or not anyone bought credits that month. With no subscription floor, a quiet month is a loss — and quiet months are not rare.

Why A fails · 3

Pay-as-you-go decays

Of 1,000 who ever bought a pack, ~620 are still active at month 12. No renewal moment, no reason to return. Subscription revenue compounds; pack revenue leaks.

The call

Ship Plan B — but steal Plan A's generosity at the very start. Give every new account a one-time 2,000-credit grant that expires in 7 days, separate from the monthly free allotment.

It costs about $4 per signup at worst, it is capped and time-boxed so it can never become the uncapped free tier that sinks Plan A, and it buys the one thing you most need: Time to First GO under eight minutes.

The one thing to protect

Through all ninety days, one rule outranks every revenue target: nobody is ever charged for something they did not knowingly start, and nobody is ever charged for something that failed.

Credits are a promise that the machine is being honest about what it costs to do work for you. Every other number in these five pages is recoverable if you get it wrong. That one is not.
